1. TARIFFS
--------------------

Tariffs don't protect your job. They send you the bill.

His argument is essentially that if you tax the foreign car, you save the British job. And Reform keep repeating that line for a reason. It gives them a foreigner to blame for everything.

The problem is, it's wrong.

He says do what the Americans have done. Fine. Let's look at what the Americans have done.

In 2018, America put tariffs on imported washing machines. And it worked. It created about eighteen hundred jobs. It also cost American shoppers one and a half billion dollars a year. That's over eight hundred thousand dollars per job. You could have paid every one of those workers a hundred grand to stay at home and still saved over a billion dollars.

And oh, by the way, the price of tumble dryers went up too. There was no tariff on dryers. Companies just knew they could.

Because a tariff isn't paid by China. It's paid by whoever is standing at the till. You.

And don't think this is just Reform. This government has put a fifty percent tariff on imported steel. The British Chambers of Commerce called it a "perfect storm" for the manufacturers who actually use the stuff.

And Britain worked this out nearly two hundred years ago. In 1815 we taxed foreign grain to protect British farming. What it actually protected was landowners' rents, and working people paid for it in the price of bread. In 1846 Robert Peel scrapped it. It split his own party and it cost him his job. And Britain became the richest trading nation on earth.

But here's the part people miss. Trade isn't a football match. Nobody has to lose. When you buy a coffee, you say thank you, and so does the person selling it. That's trade. Both sides walk away better off, or they wouldn't do it.

And when countries forget that, it gets dangerous. In 1930 America raised tariffs on twenty thousand goods. Everybody hit back. World trade fell by two thirds, and a bad crash became a far deeper depression.

Britain didn't get rich by hiding from the world. It got rich by trading with it. Free trade isn't a foreign idea. It's a British one. And I think it's time we took it back.

2. BAD APPLE
--------------------

Russia doesn't need to invade Britain. It just needs to rent a few of our politicians.

His argument is essentially that this was one man, one mistake, nothing to see here.

The problem is, that's not what the court heard.

Nathan Gill sat in the European Parliament alongside Nigel Farage. He went on to lead Reform UK in Wales. And between 2018 and 2019, he took around forty thousand pounds in bribes. Eight counts. He pleaded guilty to every one.

And what was he paid for? Scripts. A pro-Russian politician wrote the words, and Gill stood up in the European Parliament and said them. Five thousand euros for a single TV interview.

The judge said the money traced back to Viktor Medvedchuk. Vladimir Putin is godfather to his daughter.

And oh, by the way, the judge said Gill enlisted other British MEPs to take the same line.

Ten and a half years.

But here's the part people miss. Forty thousand pounds. That's it. That's what it cost to put Moscow's words in a British politician's mouth. And our own Prime Minister says the Kremlin spends over a billion pounds a year on this. Bots. Fake websites. Forged logos of the BBC and British universities.

So that's not a bad apple. That's a shopping list. And Gill is just the one who got caught.

Criticising your country is democracy. Being paid by someone else's to do it is something else.

Britain has faced down far bigger threats than a troll farm. But we have to notice it first. Because loving your country means it isn't for sale. And the people who shout loudest about patriotism should be the first to say so.

3. MIDDLE POWERS
--------------------

The world that kept Britain safe for eighty years is ending. And almost nobody in British politics will say it out loud.

That's Mark Carney. Prime Minister of Canada. He used to run the Bank of England. And he's right.

In 1941, Churchill and Roosevelt met on a warship off Newfoundland and wrote down what the world should look like after the war. No land grabs. Open trade. Free seas. That became the United Nations, NATO, and the trading system we still use.

And the deal was simple. America protects the system, and everybody else plays by the rules. For eighty years the US Navy kept the sea lanes open. Pirates, blockades, it didn't matter whose ship it was. And it worked. World trade grew more than forty times over.

So what changed? America did. It put tariffs on its own allies. And this year, American bombs started a war that all but shut the Strait of Hormuz. A fifth of the world's oil goes through it. Eighty-five ships a day became a trickle.

The navy that kept the world's waterways open helped close the most important one on the planet.

And our politicians? They know. They used to say so.

Then he won. And they stopped.

They're scared. Scared of the tariffs. Scared of the phone call. Canada shares a border with America, and its Prime Minister still said it out loud. What's our excuse?

But here's the part people miss. The middle powers aren't small. Put Britain, the European Union, Canada, Japan, Australia and South Korea together, and you've got an economy bigger than America's. Bigger than China's. They've just never had to act like it.

And it's already started. Canada has applied to join a defence force that Britain leads.

So no, the old order isn't coming back. But the things it stood for, open trade, borders that mean something, rules that apply to the big guy too, those weren't American ideas. A lot of them were ours.

Britain helped write the rules once, on a ship in the Atlantic. We can wait to find out what's on the menu. Or we can pull up a chair and write them again.

4. GUNS FOR HIRE
--------------------

When Russia sends hired guns abroad, we call them mercenaries. When America does it, we call them contractors.

Start here. Wagner. A private army working for the Kremlin. Libya. The Central African Republic. Mali. They prop up governments, and take their pay in gold and mining rights. In one Malian village, Moura, hundreds of civilians were killed in five days.

And we're right to condemn it.

But go back twenty years. Baghdad, 2007. Nisour Square. Guards from an American company called Blackwater open fire in traffic. Fourteen civilians dead. Four guards were convicted. In 2020, the President pardoned all four.

The man who founded Blackwater is Erik Prince. And he's back in business. Here.

Seven hundred miles from Miami. Port-au-Prince. Up to ninety percent of it is run by gangs. The port. Every highway out. Then they went north, into the Artibonite, where Haiti grows its rice. A million and a half people have fled.

The government can't take it back. So it hired Prince's new company, Vectus Global, with a licence from the US State Department. They fly armed drones over one of the most crowded cities in the Americas.

Human Rights Watch counted a hundred and forty-one drone operations in ten months. One thousand, two hundred and forty-three dead. Seventeen of them were children.

But here's the part people miss. Nobody elected them. And nobody can vote them out.

It isn't the same as Wagner. Haiti's government asked for this, and the gangs are real. But if a private army killing civilians in someone else's country is wrong when Moscow sends it, we should be able to say what it is when Washington signs the licence.

5. COAL AND THE VOTE
--------------------

Three hundred million years ago, a swamp decided how Britain would vote.

This is Britain. And this is what's underneath it. Coal. South Wales. Durham. Yorkshire. Nottinghamshire. Lancashire. The middle of Scotland.

Where there was coal, there were pits. By 1913, about three thousand of them, with over a million men underground.

Pits made villages. Villages made unions. And the unions built a political party.

This is the 2010 election. Labour's worst result since 1983. Take away the big cities, and look at what's left.

It's the same map.

Then the pits closed. The last deep mine shut in 2015. The vote stayed.

Until 2019. Bolsover, Labour since 1950. Sedgefield, Tony Blair's old seat. Gone.

In 2024, most of them came back. A year later, Durham, Nottinghamshire, Derbyshire and Lancashire handed their councils to Reform.

But here's the part people miss. These places didn't change sides three times in six years because they can't make up their minds. Coal gave them a reason to vote together. Nobody has given them another one.

The rock hasn't moved. The loyalty has. And whoever earns it next wins the country.

6. UN
--------------------

The next leader of the United Nations could be Viktor Orbán.

The man Hungary voted out in April, after sixteen years. The man the European Parliament said had turned his country into an "electoral autocracy".

Or it could be Lana Nusseibeh. A government minister from the United Arab Emirates, backed by Saudi Arabia and the rest of the Gulf, and criticised by Human Rights Watch over her country's record in Sudan and Yemen.

There is a frontrunner. Rebeca Grynspan of Costa Rica. In the last vote she got twelve out of fifteen. In any normal election, that's a landslide.

It isn't enough. Because five countries can each kill any candidate on their own. America. Russia. China. Britain. France.

And the Wall Street Journal reports that Trump officials have encouraged Orbán to run, as a joint pick for America, Russia and China.

Think about what the United Nations is for. It was built in 1945 on one idea. That power answers to rules. That leaders are held to account.

Now look at who'd be choosing. Vladimir Putin is wanted by the International Criminal Court. Donald Trump put sanctions on that court. Xi Jinping's government was told by the UN's own human rights office that it may have committed crimes against humanity.

None of them wants a Secretary-General who asks questions. That's the point of picking Orbán.

But here's the part people miss. Britain has a veto. So does France. Neither has used it since 1989.

Our leaders need to say it now, out loud. If three men who answer to no one try to hand the United Nations to a fourth, we will block it.

That's what the veto is for.

7. SECOND REFERENDUM
--------------------

Asking people again isn't the opposite of democracy. It's what democracy is.

His argument is essentially that the people spoke in 2016, and that's the end of it.

The problem is, he didn't believe that himself. A month before the vote, expecting to lose, Nigel Farage said this: "In a 52-48 referendum this would be unfinished business by a long way."

The result was fifty-two, forty-eight.

And Britain has done this before. We joined Europe in 1973. Two years later, we held a referendum on whether to stay. Nobody called that an insult.

Other democracies do it all the time. Ireland voted against divorce in 1986, and for it in 1995. Denmark voted no to a European treaty in 1992, and yes eleven months later, on a better deal. Switzerland said no to votes for women in 1959. It asked again.

So what's changed since 2016?

About seven million people have turned eighteen. None of them got a say. In one poll, eighty-six percent of the youngest voters would rejoin.

The three hundred and fifty million pounds a week on the side of the bus. The statistics watchdog called it a clear misuse of official statistics. The official Leave campaign was fined for breaking electoral law.

And fifty-seven percent of the country now says leaving was the wrong decision.

But here's the part people miss. A referendum isn't a verdict for ever. It's what one set of people thought, on one day, with what they'd been told.

A leading Brexiteer put it best. David Davis: "If a democracy cannot change its mind, it ceases to be a democracy."

Ten years on, the question isn't whether we're allowed to ask again. It's who's afraid of the answer.
