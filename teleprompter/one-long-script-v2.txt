1. TARIFFS
--------------------

Tariffs don't protect your job. They send you the bill.

His argument is essentially that if you tax the foreign car, you save the British job.

And Reform keep repeating that line for a reason. It gives them a foreigner to blame for everything. The problem is, it's wrong.

He says do what the Americans have done. Fine. Let's look at what the Americans have done.

In 2018, America put tariffs on imported washing machines. And it worked.

It created about eighteen hundred jobs.

It also cost American shoppers one and a half billion dollars a year.

That's over eight hundred thousand dollars per job.

You could have paid every one of those workers a hundred grand to stay at home and still saved over a billion dollars.

And oh, by the way, the price of tumble dryers went up too. There was no tariff on dryers. They're sold as a pair, so both prices went up.

Because that tariff wasn't paid by China. It was paid by whoever was standing at the till. And here, that's you.

And Britain worked this out nearly two hundred years ago. In 1815 we shut out foreign grain to protect British farming.

What it actually protected was landowners' rents, and working people paid for it in the price of bread.

In 1846 Robert Peel scrapped it. It split his own party and it cost him his job.

And Britain went on to trade with the whole world.

But here's the part people miss. Trade isn't a football match. Nobody has to lose.

When you buy a coffee, you say thank you, and so does the person selling it. That's trade.

Both sides walk away better off, or they wouldn't do it. And when countries forget that, it gets dangerous.

In 1930 America raised tariffs on twenty thousand goods. Other countries hit back.

Within four years world trade had fallen by two thirds, and most economists say it made a bad depression worse.

Britain didn't get rich by hiding from the world. It got rich by trading with it.

Free trade isn't a foreign idea. It's a British one. And I think it's time we took it back.

3. MIDDLE POWERS
--------------------

The world that kept Britain safe for eighty years is ending.

And almost nobody in British politics will say it out loud.

That's Mark Carney. Prime Minister of Canada. He used to run the Bank of England.

And he's right.

In 1941, Churchill and Roosevelt met on a warship off Newfoundland and wrote down what the world should look like after the war.

No land grabs.

Open trade.

Free seas.

That became the United Nations, NATO, and the trading system we still use.

And the deal was simple. America protects the system, and everybody else plays by the rules.

For eighty years the US Navy kept the sea lanes open.

Pirates, blockades, it didn't matter whose ship it was.

And it worked. World trade grew more than forty times over.

So what changed? America did.

It put tariffs on its own allies.

And this year, American and Israeli bombs started a war that choked the Strait of Hormuz. A fifth of the world's oil used to go through it.

Eighty-five ships a day. Nine months on, it's still a fraction of that.

The navy that kept the world's waterways open has helped close the most important one on the planet.

And our politicians? They know. They're scared. Scared of the tariffs. Scared of the phone call.

Canada shares a border with America, and its Prime Minister still said it out loud.

What's our excuse?

But here's the part people miss. The middle powers aren't small.

Put Britain, the European Union, Canada, Japan, Australia and South Korea together, and you've got an economy bigger than America's.

Bigger than China's.

They've just never had to act like it. And it's already started.

Canada has applied to join a defence force that Britain leads.

So no, the old order isn't coming back. But the things it stood for, open trade, borders that mean something, rules that apply to the big guy too, those weren't American ideas.

A lot of them were ours.

Britain helped write the rules once, on a ship in the Atlantic. We can wait to find out what's on the menu. Or we can pull up a chair and write them again.

5. COAL AND THE VOTE
--------------------

Three hundred million years ago, a swamp decided how Britain would vote.

And this is what's underneath it. Coal. South Wales. Durham. Yorkshire. Nottinghamshire. Lancashire. The middle of Scotland.

Where there was coal, there were pits. By 1913, around three thousand of them, with over a million men working them.

Pits made villages. Villages made unions. And the unions built a political party.

These are the areas which voted Labour in 2010. Labour's worst result since 1983.

Two in three of them are sitting on a coalfield.

Not every coal seat was Labour. But off the coal, Labour won one seat in four.

Then the pits closed. The last deep mine shut in 2015. The vote stayed.

Until 2019. Bolsover, Labour since 1950. Sedgefield, Tony Blair's old seat. Gone.

In 2024, most of them came back. A year later, Durham, Nottinghamshire, Derbyshire and Lancashire handed their councils to Reform.

But here's the part people miss. These places didn't change sides three times in six years because they can't make up their minds. Coal gave them a reason to vote together. Nobody has given them another one.

The rock hasn't moved. The loyalty has. And whoever earns it next wins the country.

--------------------

In July, an American pilot was shot dead on this island.

For a day, the world looked. Then it looked away.

Because this is a war you have almost certainly never heard of.

It has lasted sixty years.

This is New Guinea. This half is a country. This half is West Papua. Indonesia has run it since 1963.

In 1969 came the vote. But only one thousand and twenty-five people were allowed to take part. Indonesia chose them. They voted in front of the army. Every one of them voted to join Indonesia.
