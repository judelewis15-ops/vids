# Handoff: seven video scripts with shot lists

This document is for a Claude session picking up a short-form video project part-way through. It is self-contained: every script and its shot list is below. The person you are working with wrote none of this text themselves but directed all of it, and has read each script in chat.

## What this is

Seven draft scripts for vertical short-form videos (Instagram Reels and similar) on British and international politics, made from a pro-EU, anti-protectionist, liberal-internationalist point of view. None has been recorded. Each has a shot list, alternative hooks, a list of facts still to check, and sources.

## How far to trust the facts

Treat every factual claim as unverified until checked. The session that wrote these could run web searches but could not open most web pages, so:

- Quotes and figures marked as coming from a search are from search-result summaries, not from reading the source.
- Anything marked "from memory" was not looked up at all.
- Each script's "Facts to check before recording" section lists what is in which category. Work through it before the script is recorded, and change the script where a fact does not hold.
- Scripts 3 and 6 describe events that were still moving in November 2026 (the Strait of Hormuz, the UN Secretary-General selection). Check the current state and fix tenses.
- Scripts 2, 4 and 6 name living people alongside serious allegations. Keep the attributions exactly as careful as they are written, and read the care sections.

## The two formats

**Talking head (scripts 1, 2, 3, 6, 7).** Modelled on the American conservative commentator Tony Vanderhoef, https://www.instagram.com/tonyvanderhoef/ . Only his format is borrowed, not his politics.

**Watch the reference videos frame by frame before editing, shooting or changing a shot list.** Do not rely on the descriptions in this document or on a transcript. Step through each one and note, with timestamps: every cut and how long each shot lasts, what is on screen in each shot, which spoken word each image lands on, where the presenter is on camera and for how long, any on-screen text and how it is styled, and the first three seconds in detail. If you cannot play video, download the file and extract frames at two or more a second into contact sheets, then read those. The previous session only managed this for the Johnny Harris reel; for the Vanderhoef reels it had an automated scene description, which did not report captions, zooms or music, so those are still unknown and are yours to establish.

The reference videos:
- Rebuttal model for scripts 1, 2 and 7: his reply to Abdul El-Sayed on billionaires, https://www.instagram.com/p/DeK_e8DsUVd/ (532k views). Broad verdict, then the opponent's clip, then "His argument is essentially…", "The problem is, it's wrong", numbers, and "Who do you trust?"
- Thesis model for scripts 3 and 6: "What the hell happened to the Democratic Party?", https://www.instagram.com/reel/DSizjtUDG2U/ (1.09M views). Question, then versus now, "So what changed?", "But here's the part people miss", moral close.
- Picture and pace model: "I'm an Eisenhower Republican", https://www.instagram.com/reel/Db53VzmRHy6/ (1.35M views, his most viewed). About 25 cuts a minute, about 78% archive and graphics, one image per name or number, charts with the figure circled.
- Fastest cutting, present-day footage: the World Cup reel, https://www.instagram.com/reel/DZ0i_xLRXTl/ (834k views, 46 seconds, about 31 cuts a minute).
- Policy takedown with hard numbers: the "Mamdani Markets" reel, https://www.instagram.com/reel/DbekT9otmJJ/ (987k views).
- What to avoid: his Ground News sponsor read, https://www.instagram.com/reel/DY5fbZBxgH2/ (29k views). Abstract topic, and it holds on the presenter for 10 to 13 seconds at a time.

View counts are from a scrape on 10 November 2026. The picture and pace figures come from an automated scene analysis, so treat them as approximate.
- Script shape: a verdict or question in line one; for rebuttals, a clip of the opponent straight after, then "His argument is essentially…" and "The problem is…"; a hard number early; then versus now; "But here's the part people miss"; an optimistic or patriotic close said to camera.
- Picture: presenter to camera for the first two seconds, then cut away. One image per name or number, held only as long as the phrase. Mostly full-screen archive photos and footage, simple line charts with the key figure circled, screenshots of headlines. Never hold on the presenter for more than about five seconds mid-video. End on the presenter.
- Pace: roughly 17 to 30 cuts a minute. A short sound effect on image changes.
- Delivery: about 160 to 190 words a minute, short plain sentences.

**Map-led (scripts 4, 5).** Modelled on Johnny Harris's reel "How the Nile created a civilization" (https://www.instagram.com/p/DfaOC6qnHIv/).
- A tilted 3D relief map with warm low light, haze, shallow depth of field, drifting clouds, glowing city lights and old-atlas serif labels.
- The camera never stops: slow push-ins and drifts held for 5 to 12 seconds. One new element per beat. Lines and fills animate on.
- Archive material sits in a rounded frame on black. Captions are small white monospace on a grey box, low in frame, a phrase at a time.
- Far fewer words than the talking-head scripts.
- Watch this one frame by frame too, the same way, before building any map animation. The description above came from stepping through it at two frames a second without sound, so the audio, music and exact timing of the voice against the picture have not been studied.
- A full build brief for script 5 exists in the repository at `handoff/coal-map-after-effects.md`.

## Recording setup

- Camera: Sony a6400 with a zoom lens, mounted vertically. Suggested: 4K at 25 frames a second, shutter 1/50, aperture wide open, about 40 to 50 mm, presenter 1.2 to 1.5 m in front of the backdrop.
- Light: two 50 × 70 cm softboxes with 45W dimmable LED heads (3000K to 6600K). One as a key at 45 degrees; the other low and warm on the backdrop.
- Backdrop: a bookcase with a solid dark back, a globe, a small lamp and framed pictures. Not yet bought or built; a CASART S-shaped bookcase was being considered.
- Sound: not yet bought. A USB microphone cannot plug into the a6400, which takes a 3.5 mm plug.
- Output: 1080 × 1920.

## Script 1: Tariffs don't protect your job

Source file: `scripts/01-protectionism-tariffs.md`

Status: draft script, not yet recorded. Facts below still need checking.
Format: rebuttal reel (broad verdict, clip, "the problem is, it's wrong", numbers, history, turn, optimistic close).
Target length: about 105 seconds, roughly 320 words, about 20 cuts a minute.

### Script

Tariffs don't protect your job. They send you the bill.

**[CLIP: Richard Tice]** "Unless we put 100 per cent tariffs on all Chinese electric vehicles as the Americans have done, otherwise we will not have a car industry in five to seven years."

His argument is essentially that if you tax the foreign car, you save the British job. And Reform keep repeating that line for a reason. It gives them a foreigner to blame for everything.

The problem is, it's wrong.

He says do what the Americans have done. Fine. Let's look at what the Americans have done.

In 2018, America put tariffs on imported washing machines. And it worked. It created about eighteen hundred jobs. It also cost American shoppers one and a half billion dollars a year. That's over eight hundred thousand dollars per job. You could have paid every one of those workers a hundred grand to stay at home and still saved over a billion dollars.

And oh, by the way, the price of tumble dryers went up too. There was no tariff on dryers. Companies just knew they could.

Because a tariff isn't paid by China. It's paid by whoever is standing at the till. You.

*(Optional, adds about 10 seconds)* And don't think this is just Reform. This government has put a fifty percent tariff on imported steel. The British Chambers of Commerce called it a "perfect storm" for the manufacturers who actually use the stuff.

And Britain worked this out nearly two hundred years ago. In 1815 we taxed foreign grain to protect British farming. What it actually protected was landowners' rents, and working people paid for it in the price of bread. In 1846 Robert Peel scrapped it. It split his own party and it cost him his job. And Britain became the richest trading nation on earth.

But here's the part people miss. Trade isn't a football match. Nobody has to lose. When you buy a coffee, you say thank you, and so does the person selling it. That's trade. Both sides walk away better off, or they wouldn't do it.

And when countries forget that, it gets dangerous. In 1930 America raised tariffs on twenty thousand goods. Everybody hit back. World trade fell by two thirds, and a bad crash became a far deeper depression.

Britain didn't get rich by hiding from the world. It got rich by trading with it. Free trade isn't a foreign idea. It's a British one. And I think it's time we took it back.

### Alternative hooks

- "Britain invented free trade. So why are we being told to forget it?"
- "It cost America eight hundred thousand dollars to save one job."
- "A tariff is a tax. On you."

### Facts to check before recording

- **Tice quote.** Reported by GB News, date estimated as mid-September 2026 from the search result's age. The wording here is from a search summary, not the page itself. Confirm the exact words and find the footage.
- **Washing machines.** About 1,800 jobs, about $1.5bn a year in consumer cost, roughly $815,000 per job, dryer prices rising alongside. Source: Flaaen, Hortaçsu and Tintelnot, American Economic Review, 2020. Written from memory, not looked up.

- **Steel.** Announced 19 March 2026: quotas cut 60% from 1 July, 50% tariff above them. Not confirmed what the final terms were once it took effect; the Chambers of Commerce later welcomed some quota amendments. Check before saying "has put".

## Script 2: That's not a bad apple. That's a shopping list.

Source file: `scripts/02-russian-influence-nathan-gill.md`

Status: draft script, not yet recorded. Facts below still need checking.
Format: rebuttal reel (broad verdict, clip, "the problem is…", court facts, scale, turn, patriotic close).

### Script

Russia doesn't need to invade Britain. It just needs to rent a few of our politicians.

**[CLIP: Nigel Farage]** "Every single political party has a bad apple. In fact, many families finish up with a bad apple, these things happen."

His argument is essentially that this was one man, one mistake, nothing to see here.

The problem is, that's not what the court heard.

Nathan Gill sat in the European Parliament alongside Nigel Farage. He went on to lead Reform UK in Wales. And between 2018 and 2019, he took around forty thousand pounds in bribes. Eight counts. He pleaded guilty to every one.

And what was he paid for? Scripts. A pro-Russian politician wrote the words, and Gill stood up in the European Parliament and said them. Five thousand euros for a single TV interview.

The judge said the money traced back to Viktor Medvedchuk. Vladimir Putin is godfather to his daughter.

Ten and a half years.

But here's the part people miss. Forty thousand pounds. That's it. That's what it cost to put Moscow's words in a British politician's mouth. And our own Prime Minister says the Kremlin spends over a billion pounds a year on this. Bots. Fake websites. Forged logos of the BBC and British universities.

So that's not a bad apple. That's a shopping list. And Gill is just the one who got caught.

Criticising your country is democracy. Being paid by someone else's to do it is something else.

Britain has faced down far bigger threats than a troll farm. But we have to notice it first. Because loving your country means it isn't for sale. And the people who shout loudest about patriotism should be the first to say so.

## Script 3: At the table, or on the menu

Source file: `scripts/03-liberal-world-order-middle-powers.md`

Status: draft script, not yet recorded. Facts below still need checking.
Format: thesis reel (broad verdict, supporting clip, then versus now, what changed, turn, patriotic close).
Target length: about 115 seconds, roughly 340 words plus two clips, about 20 to 25 cuts a minute.

### Script

The world that kept Britain safe for eighty years is ending. And almost nobody in British politics will say it out loud.

**[CLIP: Mark Carney, Davos, January 2026]** "We are in the midst of a rupture, not a transition… Middle powers must act together, because if we're not at the table, we're on the menu."

That's Mark Carney. Prime Minister of Canada. He used to run the Bank of England. And he's right.

In 1941, Churchill and Roosevelt met on a warship off Newfoundland and wrote down what the world should look like after the war. No land grabs. Open trade. Free seas. That became the United Nations, NATO, and the trading system we still use.

And the deal was simple. America protects the system, and everybody else plays by the rules. For eighty years the US Navy kept the sea lanes open. Pirates, blockades, it didn't matter whose ship it was. And it worked. World trade grew more than forty times over.

So what changed? America did. It put tariffs on its own allies. And this year, American bombs started a war that all but shut the Strait of Hormuz. A fifth of the world's oil goes through it. Eighty-five ships a day became a trickle.

The navy that kept the world's waterways open helped close the most important one on the planet.

And our politicians? They know. They used to say so.

**[ON SCREEN: James Cleverly's 2015 tweet, 2 to 3 seconds]** "Dear Republican colleagues, please, please, please don't choose Donald Trump…"

Then he won. And they stopped.

**[CLIP: the dodge, 4 to 5 seconds. Candidate: Cleverly in 2024 refusing to say whether he would vote for Trump.]**

They're scared. Scared of the tariffs. Scared of the phone call. Canada shares a border with America, and its Prime Minister still said it out loud. What's our excuse?

But here's the part people miss. The middle powers aren't small. Put Britain, the European Union, Canada, Japan, Australia and South Korea together, and you've got an economy bigger than America's. Bigger than China's. They've just never had to act like it.

And it's already started. Canada has applied to join a defence force that Britain leads.

So no, the old order isn't coming back. But the things it stood for, open trade, borders that mean something, rules that apply to the big guy too, those weren't American ideas. A lot of them were ours.

Britain helped write the rules once, on a ship in the Atlantic. We can wait to find out what's on the menu. Or we can pull up a chair and write them again.

### Does the argument hold up?

Mostly yes, with four places an opponent will push.

1. **"So soon" is the weakest part of their case.** It has been ten years. Britain's own precedent is two.
2. **The Irish and Danish re-votes cut both ways.** Eurosceptics use the same examples to say the EU makes countries vote until they give the right answer. The script leads with Britain's own 1975 vote and uses the Irish divorce and Swiss examples, which have nothing to do with the EU, for that reason. Denmark is included because it re-voted on a changed deal, which is the honest parallel.
3. **Scotland.** Many of the people who want a second EU referendum opposed a second Scottish independence referendum on the grounds that 2014 was "once in a generation". Expect that in the comments. The consistent position is that either can be asked again when circumstances change.
4. **Rejoin support is softer than the headline.** YouGov's 55% for rejoining in principle falls to 35% if rejoining means giving up the old opt-outs, and an Opinium poll in May 2026 found only 36% agreeing the UK should rejoin. The script uses "wrong decision" (57%), which is the stronger and more defensible number, and does not claim a rejoin vote would be won.

Two things deliberately left out:
- **The "Leave voters have died" argument.** Peter Kellner estimates about 3.8 million Leave voters and 2.1 million Remain voters have died since 2016. It is an estimate by a pro-EU analyst, the statistics office says it lacks the data, and it lands badly. "Seven million have turned eighteen" makes the same point without it.
- **Any claim that the 2016 result was invalid.** The script says the campaign misled and broke spending law, not that the vote should not have counted. Remain's forecasts were also criticised.
