# Asking again isn't undemocratic

Status: draft script, not yet recorded. Facts below still need checking.
Format: talking-head rebuttal reel (broad verdict, clip, "the problem is…", precedents, what has changed, turn, close).
Target length: about 110 seconds, roughly 320 words.
Researched 12 November 2026.

## Script

Asking people again isn't the opposite of democracy. It's what democracy is.

**[CLIP: Nigel Farage calling a second referendum an insult to the 17.4 million who voted Leave]**

His argument is essentially that the people spoke in 2016, and that's the end of it.

The problem is, he didn't believe that himself. A month before the vote, expecting to lose, Nigel Farage said this: "In a 52-48 referendum this would be unfinished business by a long way."

The result was fifty-two, forty-eight.

And Britain has done this before. We joined Europe in 1973. Two years later, we held a referendum on whether to stay. Nobody called that an insult.

Other democracies do it all the time. Ireland voted against divorce in 1986, and for it in 1995. Denmark voted no to a European treaty in 1992, and yes eleven months later, on a better deal. Switzerland said no to votes for women in 1959. It asked again.

So what's changed since 2016?

About seven million people have turned eighteen. None of them got a say. In one poll, eighty-six percent of the youngest voters would rejoin.

The three hundred and fifty million pounds a week on the side of the bus. The statistics watchdog called it a clear misuse of official statistics. The official Leave campaign was fined for breaking electoral law.

And fifty-seven percent of the country now says leaving was the wrong decision.

But here's the part people miss. A referendum isn't a verdict for ever. It's what one set of people thought, on one day, with what they'd been told.

A leading Brexiteer put it best. David Davis: "If a democracy cannot change its mind, it ceases to be a democracy."

Ten years on, the question isn't whether we're allowed to ask again. It's who's afraid of the answer.

## Alternative hooks

- "Nigel Farage said a 52-48 result would be unfinished business. He was right."
- "Seven million people have turned eighteen since the Brexit vote. Nobody has asked them."
- "Britain held a referendum on Europe two years after joining. Why is ten years too soon?"

## Does the argument hold up?

Mostly yes, with four places an opponent will push.

1. **"So soon" is the weakest part of their case.** It has been ten years. Britain's own precedent is two.
2. **The Irish and Danish re-votes cut both ways.** Eurosceptics use the same examples to say the EU makes countries vote until they give the right answer. The script leads with Britain's own 1975 vote and uses the Irish divorce and Swiss examples, which have nothing to do with the EU, for that reason. Denmark is included because it re-voted on a changed deal, which is the honest parallel.
3. **Scotland.** Many of the people who want a second EU referendum opposed a second Scottish independence referendum on the grounds that 2014 was "once in a generation". Expect that in the comments. The consistent position is that either can be asked again when circumstances change.
4. **Rejoin support is softer than the headline.** YouGov's 55% for rejoining in principle falls to 35% if rejoining means giving up the old opt-outs, and an Opinium poll in May 2026 found only 36% agreeing the UK should rejoin. The script uses "wrong decision" (57%), which is the stronger and more defensible number, and does not claim a rejoin vote would be won.

Two things deliberately left out:
- **The "Leave voters have died" argument.** Peter Kellner estimates about 3.8 million Leave voters and 2.1 million Remain voters have died since 2016. It is an estimate by a pro-EU analyst, the statistics office says it lacks the data, and it lands badly. "Seven million have turned eighteen" makes the same point without it.
- **Any claim that the 2016 result was invalid.** The script says the campaign misled and broke spending law, not that the vote should not have counted. Remain's forecasts were also criticised.

## Facts to check before recording

- **The Farage clip.** A recent report says he called Andy Burnham's suggestion of a second referendum an insult to the 17.4 million who voted Leave. The outlet is minor and the date and wording are unconfirmed. Find the footage or the original report.
- **Farage, 2016.** "In a 52-48 referendum this would be unfinished business by a long way", to the Mirror, May 2016, when he was talking about a narrow Remain win. From memory. He has since said he does not want a second referendum.
- **1973 and 1975.** Britain joined the European Economic Community on 1 January 1973; the referendum was 5 June 1975. From memory. The 1975 vote followed a change of government and a renegotiation; say so if challenged.
- **Ireland.** Divorce referendums in 1986 (rejected) and 1995 (narrowly approved). From memory.
- **Denmark.** Maastricht Treaty rejected in June 1992, approved in May 1993 after opt-outs were agreed. From memory.
- **Switzerland.** Men voted against women's suffrage at federal level in 1959 and for it in 1971. From memory.
- **Seven million.** Peter Kellner's estimate of Britons reaching voting age since 2016. Not an official figure.
- **86%.** A Deltapoll survey for the Mirror: 86% of 18 to 24 year olds who would vote said they would vote to rejoin; 58% overall. Date not confirmed, reported in early 2026.
- **57%.** YouGov, reported 23 June 2026: 57% say the UK was wrong to vote to leave, 30% right.
- **The bus.** From memory: in September 2017 the chair of the UK Statistics Authority wrote that the £350 million figure was "a clear misuse of official statistics". Confirm the wording.
- **The fine.** From memory: the Electoral Commission fined Vote Leave £61,000 in July 2018 for breaking spending limits.
- **David Davis.** "If a democracy cannot change its mind, it ceases to be a democracy", in a speech on Europe in November 2012. From memory. He was arguing for a referendum on leaving; that is the point.
- **Burnham's position.** Reports say he has suggested a second referendum would be needed to rejoin while saying he respects the 2016 result. Not confirmed from a major outlet.
